One worry at a time

You hand it over.
Then you hover.

Everybody does, the first time. Here is each thing you are watching for, and what happens to it — on the real screens, not a diagram of them.

They tap everything.
Nothing breaks.

Every key, both mouse buttons, the same corner forty times. There is no address bar to type into, no tab to close, no window to drag off the edge and no file to delete. The worst thing a small child can do here is open a different one of the sites you chose.

Your work stays exactly where you left it.

They will try to get out.
Three seconds says no.

Not a PIN they watch you type. Not a swipe they learn in a week. A steady three-second press, which is simply not a motion a toddler makes — they tap, and a tap does nothing at all.

If yours ever works it out, swap it for a small sum and the hold stops working entirely.

Go on — press it and keep pressing.

“Five more minutes.”
Not your argument any more.

You decide the length once, on a quiet afternoon, and it starts again every time the app opens. Then the laptop is the one that says when — and a child who is cross about it is cross with the screen, which is a much better place for it than cross with you.

No countdown ticking away to stare at, either. It appears in the last thirty seconds and not before.

Still yours to overrule: fifteen minutes, an hour, or the rest of the day — without moving your usual limit.

1,284requests refused in one afternoon

Nothing is trying to sell
to your three-year-old.

Advert and tracker requests are cut before they load, on every site you add. No pop-ups, no interstitial begging for a tap, no profile being built out of what a small child clicked.

A handful of sites ask in their terms that you do not filter them. You can leave those alone per site — your child still cannot wander off them either way.

Nobody else picks what is next.
You already did.

No autoplay into something you have not seen. No recommendation rail. No next video chosen by an algorithm that has never met your child. The tiles are the ones you put there, and the list stops where you stopped it.

Hide one for a week without deleting it. Change a name, a colour, the shape on the tile. Quit from the corner, where it belongs.

Adding a site takes a minute.
Not an afternoon.

Sukhi Play opens the site, watches what it actually needs, and fills in the rest — a name, a picture, a colour, the list of hosts to allow. You see all of it and change anything before you save.

Or take one from the catalogue, already set up. Three of those are not websites at all: a colouring book, a keyboard playground and a set of first games that come inside the download and work on a train.

And the small print, which is short

  • Nothing downloads itselfA site cannot open a window, start a download, or ask for your camera. No mystery installs in your Downloads folder on Sunday night.
  • The keyboard is safe to bashAlt+Tab, Alt+F4, Command+Q, Print Screen — held down while the window has focus. The Windows key is the one exception and we say so on the Windows page, rather than hoping you do not notice.
  • Nothing is sent anywhereNo sign-in, no account, no analytics, no telemetry, no crash reports. The only request Sukhi Play makes on its own is checking for a newer version, and only when you press the button.
  • It costs nothing, and always willMIT licensed and open source. Every line is public, including the parts that decide what your child can reach.
