The World Health Organization says children aged two to four should have no more than one hour of sedentary screen time a day, and that less is better. The American Academy of Pediatrics gives the same hour for ages two to five, of high-quality programmes, watched together where possible. What made that workable for me was less the number than the shape: short sessions, the same calm stop every time, and sites that do not shout.

This is one parent's experience, not medical advice. If you are worried about your child, your GP or health visitor is the person to ask.

## What does the WHO say about screen time for a three-year-old?

The WHO's 2019 guidelines for children under five put it simply. For ages two to four, sedentary screen time should be no more than one hour a day, and less is better. For children under two it is not recommended at all.

The guidance is as much about moving, playing and sleeping as it is about screens. Screens are counted as time spent sitting still, and the message is to sit less and play more. The [WHO news release](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more) is short and worth reading in full.

## What does the American Academy of Pediatrics recommend?

For children aged two to five, the AAP suggests limiting screen use to about one hour a day of high-quality programming, and watching it with them where you can, so you can talk about what they are seeing. Their [advice for parents](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/Where-We-Stand-TV-Viewing-Time.aspx) is on HealthyChildren.org.

## Is one hour of screen time too much for a three-year-old?

An hour is a ceiling, not a target. Both sets of guidance say less is better, and neither says a child needs screens at all.

What matters as much in practice is what the time looks like. Twenty calm minutes with a grown-up nearby is a very different thing from an hour of something fast and loud, alone.

## Is it better to have one long session or a few short ones?

Short sessions, for a child this age. A three-year-old has no idea what "twenty minutes left today" means. A session, on the other hand, has a beginning and an end they can see: they sit down, they play, the screen says it is finished, they get up.

It also makes the stop predictable. If every session is the same length, the end stops being a surprise and starts being a routine.

## How do I warn a toddler that screen time is nearly over?

With your voice, tied to something they understand. "When this picture is finished" or "after this one" lands better than a number.

A countdown on the screen tends to do the opposite of what you want. It gives a child something to watch, and something to bargain with. That is why Sukhi Play shows no countdown at all until the last thirty seconds.

## How do I end screen time without a meltdown?

There is no trick that works every time, but these helped:

1. **Let the stop come from the screen, not from you.** A stop screen that simply says play is over is hard to argue with, because there is nobody to argue with.
2. **Keep the length the same every time.** Consistency does most of the work.
3. **Have the next thing ready.** A snack, the garden, the bath. Ending is easier when it is also starting something.
4. **Do not give more time on demand.** If you do give more, make it clearly your decision, not the result of a good argument.
5. **Sit with them when you can.** Both for the reasons the AAP gives, and because you see the stop coming.

## Which sites are calmer for small children?

Look for things a child makes, rather than things that play at them:

- no autoplay, and no "up next"
- no adverts
- no timers, streaks or flashing rewards
- nothing that needs reading to get started

Colouring, drawing, building and simple music toys tend to end more gently than videos, because there is no cliffhanger. If your child does watch videos, YouTube Kids is a better starting point than YouTube, and has parental settings of its own.

## What free tools can end a session for me?

Several are built in. On an iPad, Guided Access has a time limit option, and Screen Time can set limits on Apple devices. On Windows, Microsoft's family settings can limit screen time on a child's account, and Google Family Link does the same on Android.

## How does Sukhi Play's play time work?

On a laptop or desktop, [Sukhi Play](/download/) does this as part of the locked-down screen. On the grown-up screen's **Play time** tab, you set a session length once: any number of minutes up to four hours, or no limit. The clock starts again every time the app opens.

They can ask for more, but only a grown-up can answer: fifteen minutes, an hour, or the rest of the day, for that session only. Your usual limit does not move.

It is honest to say it counts a session, not a day. Close it and open it again after lunch and the clock starts fresh, so keeping the daily total is still your job.
